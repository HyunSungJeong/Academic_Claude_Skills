\section*{Summary}

\file{pdf-to-markdown} is a Claude skill that converts PDFs of physics papers,
notes and textbooks into files for a Claude Project: a text-only Markdown file,
named exactly like the original PDF, in which every equation is written in
LaTeX, and one companion PDF holding the document's figures. Every formula is
transcribed from page images rather than from the PDF's text layer, then checked
mechanically and proofread against the original.

Section~\ref{sec:why} analyses what this gains over uploading the original PDF.
In short: about 20--30\% less Project capacity per paper with all figures
included, and up to 40--45\% without them; exact equations instead of a garbled
text layer or small page images; content that remains searchable when a large
Project switches to retrieval; and, the largest effect, no image-reading work
whenever a conversation refers to equations, which saves an estimated
20{,}000--47{,}000 tokens in an equation-heavy chat. A conversion costs about
35{,}000--45{,}000 tokens for an eight-page paper once, so it pays for itself
within one or two such chats.

All figures in this note are estimates unless stated as measured. The evidence
comes from born-digital documents; transcription has been tested on
single-column documents only (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\section{What the skill does}

A conversion runs in six steps, all described in \file{SKILL.md}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item \file{prepare.py} cuts every page into 170\,dpi strips at the gaps between
  lines and prints, per page, the strips with their positions and the printed
  equation numbers. \file{figures.py} finds the figures and writes crop-check
  images.
\item Claude views the strips and writes each page as Markdown to its own file,
  zooming to 500--600\,dpi wherever a bar, tilde, prime, typeface or brace
  extent is in doubt.
\item Writing conventions: printed equation numbers in \texttt{\textbackslash
  tag\{\}}; typefaces exactly as printed ($\mathcal K$, $\mathrm K$,
  $\bm\Omega$, upright $\mathrm i$, $\mathrm d$, $\mathrm e$ where so printed);
  a placeholder for each figure followed by its transcribed caption; paragraphs
  joined across page breaks.
\item \file{assemble.py} joins the pages, writes the two output files, and
  \file{check.py} tests every formula in KaTeX, the equation numbers against the
  PDF, and the absence of text-layer debris.
\item \file{verify\_eqs.py} re-typesets each numbered equation under the PDF's
  original for a symbol-by-symbol proofread.
\item Claude delivers \file{NAME.md} and, if there are figures,
  \file{NAME\_figures.pdf}.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Output naming.} \texttt{NAME} is the original PDF's file name without
\texttt{.pdf}, unchanged: same characters, case, spaces and underscores. In
\file{NAME.md} each figure appears as a line such as
\texttt{*[Fig.\ 4: NAME\_figures.pdf, page 1]*} followed by its caption, so
Claude knows where to look.

\section{Why Markdown instead of the original PDF}
\label{sec:why}

All numbers in this section are estimates for the eight-page, two-column PNAS
paper used in testing (Lihm, Kiese, Lee \& Kugler, PNAS \textbf{123},
e2525308123, 2026), based on Anthropic's published approximation for image
tokens (width $\times$ height $/750$ after scaling to at most 1{,}568\,px and
about 1.15 megapixels) and on character counts for text. None is a metered
measurement.

\subsection{What Claude receives}

From a PDF, Claude receives the extracted text and, for each page, an image.
The text layer garbles mathematics: calligraphic and roman letters merge, bold
is lost, accents detach (in testing, $\tilde{\mathrm K}=1/\Omega_{\bar 1}$ came
out as \texttt{K = 1 \~{} /}$\Omega$\texttt{\textasciimacron 1}), and big operators
become placeholder codes. A full page squeezed into one image is seen at only
about 111--114\,dpi, close to where subscripts, bars and primes became uncertain
in the legibility test (Section~\ref{sec:tests}). From the Markdown, Claude
receives every equation as exact LaTeX text, with no reading involved.

\subsection{Project capacity}

\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}Lrr@{}}
\toprule
Uploaded per paper & Tokens & Papers in $\sim$200K \\
\midrule
Original PDF (text layer $\sim$15--19K + 8 page images $\sim$12K) & 27{,}000--31{,}000 & 5--6 \\
\file{NAME.md} + \file{NAME\_figures.pdf} (3 pages, $\sim$3{,}600) & 19{,}000--24{,}000 & 7--9 \\
\file{NAME.md} only & 15{,}000--20{,}000 & 8--11 \\
\file{NAME.md} without the reference list & 10{,}000--14{,}000 & 12--16 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}

The text costs about the same in both forms (58{,}000 characters for this
paper); the saving comes from replacing eight full-page images by three pages of
figures, or by none. With all figures included the Markdown route uses about
20--30\% less capacity; without the figures PDF, about 40--45\% less. Leaving
out the reference list, a third of this paper's text, saves more than any choice
about figures. Project knowledge is read into every conversation of the Project,
so the same percentages apply to the standing cost of each chat.

When a Project's knowledge approaches the context limit, Claude switches to
retrieval and searches the files instead of reading them whole. Clean LaTeX and
text are found by such a search; the content of page images is not.

\subsection{Working with equations in a chat}

Reading an equation reliably from a PDF means the careful route of the
academic-pdf-reading skill: its instructions (measured: about 5{,}200 tokens),
a triage of the file, locating the equation, and a cropped, enlarged image of it
for every equation, followed by a transcription.

\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}Lrr@{}}
\toprule
Extra cost & Image route & Markdown \\
\midrule
Setup per chat (instructions, triage) & $\sim$6{,}000--7{,}000 & 0 \\
Per equation question, new tokens & $\sim$1{,}500--4{,}000 & $\sim$0 \\
Per equation question, tool round trips & 4--7 & 0 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}

The crop images themselves are cheap (about 100--250 tokens); the cost is the
work around them. Two scenarios:

\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}Lrrr@{}}
\toprule
Scenario & Extra new tokens & Round trips & Cached re-reads \\
\midrule
10 equation questions in one chat & 21{,}000--47{,}000 & 40--70 & 1.5--2.5\,M \\
10 equations referred to 10 times each, each read once and reused & 21{,}000--47{,}000 & 40--70 & 1.5--2.5\,M \\
the same, each re-read from the image every time & 95{,}000--270{,}000 & 220--340 & 9--14\,M \\
Markdown in the Project, any of the above & $\sim$0 & 0 & 0 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}

Reusing an equation once read is what a careful run does, but long chats are
summarised automatically near the context limit, which can discard the crops and
force re-reading. Every round trip re-reads the conversation; cached reads count
less towards usage limits than new tokens, but not nothing. In an
equation-heavy chat the saving is therefore three to six times the standing
capacity saving of a paper, and it grows with the length of the chat.

\subsection{Accuracy}

The Markdown is verified once, at conversion, with checks and proofreading.
Reading from the PDF repeats the risk of a misreading in every chat, and a
misread equation that is reused carries the error into every later reference.

\subsection{Break-even}

A conversion costs about 4{,}000--5{,}500 tokens per page, about
35{,}000--45{,}000 for an eight-page paper, once. That is roughly one
equation-heavy chat on the image route, so the conversion pays for itself within
one or two such chats and keeps paying afterwards.

\subsection{Caveats}

\begin{itemize}
\item Whether PDFs in Project knowledge are given page images, as PDFs attached
  in a chat are, has not been verified. If Projects use only the extracted text,
  the original PDF takes about as much capacity as the Markdown alone: no saving
  in capacity, but garbled equations and invisible figures remain.
\item Chats that are mostly conceptual and rarely touch equations gain little
  beyond the capacity saving.
\item Two-column papers such as the PNAS test paper cannot yet be converted
  reliably as text (Section~\ref{sec:limits}); for them the comparison above
  becomes available once column support is built.
\item Before relying on the numbers, compare the usage panel before and after
  one chat of each kind.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Recommendations}

Upload \file{NAME.md} for every paper. Add \file{NAME\_figures.pdf} only for
papers whose figures you will discuss; the captions are always in the text.
Consider removing the reference list from papers whose references you do not
need Claude to read.

\section{Figures and adaptive packing}

\textbf{Detection.} \file{figures.py} anchors each figure on its caption (a text
block starting ``Fig.~N'', ``Figure~N'' or ``FIG.~N''). The figure is everything
graphical above the caption within its column, up to the nearest running text.
The region is rendered whole at 200\,dpi, because physics figures are usually
composites of vector art and small embedded images such as heatmaps, which
extraction would split into fragments. Decorative graphics without a caption are
ignored. A check image with the crop outlined in red is viewed for every figure.

\textbf{Packing.} Claude sees a PDF page as one image of at most about
1.15 megapixels, roughly 1{,}500 tokens. More figures per page cost fewer tokens
each but get fewer pixels. \file{assemble.py} places figures at native size in
order of appearance and starts a new page when the next figure would push the
page below 150\,dpi effective: an area of about 50 square inches or a long edge
of about 10 inches. 150\,dpi is the level at which small labels stayed reliably
legible in testing. A large figure shares its page with at most one small one; a
figure too large for any page goes alone and is reported. Each figure is labelled
``Fig.~N'', and the Markdown names its page.

\midrule
2 & 4 & 190--205 & $\le$5{,}800 \\

4 & 2 & 150--155 & $\le$3{,}100 \\

\midrule

The adaptive rule packed the seven figures 4, 2 and 1 per page: adding the
seventh to page~2 would have taken that page below the floor. Bundling does not
reduce tokens below separate images of equal legibility (the seven figures as
200\,dpi PNGs: about 3{,}300 tokens); its value is a single file that a Project
accepts.

\section{Test results}
\label{sec:tests}

\textbf{Documents.} Kugler note (five pages, single column, with LaTeX source);
duality note (ten pages, single column, with source); PNAS paper (eight pages,
two columns, no source). All transcriptions were made from images before the
source was opened.

\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}Lr@{}}
\toprule
Test & Result \\
\midrule
Kugler note, whole document & 25/26 displays, 143/147 inline exact; prose identical \\
Kugler note, page 3 (densest notation) & 8/8 displays, 38/38 inline exact \\
Duality note, pages 4 and 6, open blind at 170\,dpi & 10/10 displays, 70/70 inline, strict \\
Blind legibility, 16 items $\times$ 6 resolutions (232--100\,dpi) & 96/96 correct (forced choice) \\
Proofreading, 9 planted errors & 6 caught on sheets, 3 by compile check, 0 missed \\
Scorer, 4 planted errors & 4/4 flagged; spelling variants forgiven \\
Figures (PNAS): found, crops complete, decorations ignored & 7/7, 7/7, 0 false \\
Figure captions (PNAS) in KaTeX & 48/48 \\
Figures PDF, most crowded page viewed at $\sim$151\,dpi & all labels legible \\
Output names, including \texttt{My Paper (2026) v2.PDF} & exact; references correct \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}

The five whole-document mismatches in the Kugler note render identically to the
source (spelling-only differences such as \verb|F_{\rm rest}| versus
\verb|F_{\mathrm{rest}}|). In the legibility test, about five readings felt
uncertain at 120 and 100\,dpi and none at 145\,dpi or above apart from one
overbar extent. On the most crowded figures page, colorbar exponents, inset tick
labels, the superscripts of $i_{\mathrm D}^{\mathrm{DMFT}}$ and
$\tilde I_{\mathrm D}^{\mathbf q=\Gamma}$ and the legend subscripts
$\mathbf k_{\mathrm N}$, $\mathbf k_{\mathrm{HS}}$, $\mathbf k_{\mathrm{AN}}$ were
all read without zooming. Caption accuracy of the PNAS paper was not scored,
because its source was not available. Marker could not be compared: its model
weights are hosted on sites Claude's sandbox cannot reach; a page-3 comparison
kit is provided for a local run.

\section{Cost of a conversion}

Per full page: strips about 2{,}400 tokens, Markdown output about 1{,}200,
proofreading about 380 per numbered equation, zooms 0--500; in total about
4{,}000--5{,}500, plus about 1{,}400 for \file{SKILL.md} once per conversion.
Every strip stays in the conversation, so a ten-page note leaves about
40{,}000--55{,}000 tokens in context: convert one paper or chapter per
conversation, in a fresh chat. Against a Pro plan's five-hour window, community
estimates put this at very roughly 1--4\% per page with Opus; Anthropic publishes
no token figure for the limit.

\section{Changes considered and not made}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Selective proofreading (rejected).} Errors were found in the
  plainest equations, and only 1 of 26 equations in the Kugler note would have
  been skippable.
\item \textbf{Skipping strips without math fonts (deferred).} Saves 0--6\% of
  strip tokens, and first needs scan detection and hyphen repair.
\item \textbf{Images embedded in the Markdown (removed).} Claude cannot see a
  base64 image in a text file, and it costs an estimated 100 times the tokens of
  the same image as a file.
\end{itemize}

\section{Known limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Scanned PDFs (serious).} A scan has no fonts, so \file{prepare.py}
  marks every page \texttt{text-ok}; scanned pages must be transcribed from
  images regardless.
\item \textbf{Two-column layouts.} Strips run across both columns; equation
  numbers are found only in the right quarter of the page; proofreading crops
  include the other column. Figures are not affected.
\item \textbf{Figures.} Found only when a caption below them starts with
  ``Fig.'', ``Figure'' or ``FIG.''; other cases need a manual crop. Text inside
  figures is part of the image. In a Project, Claude sees figures only if the
  figures PDF is uploaded.
\item \textbf{Dotted equation numbers} such as (5.40) are not recognised, so
  \file{check.py} wrongly fails on them.
\item \textbf{Proofreading sheets} omit equations that do not compile, merge
  adjacent \texttt{align} lines, and can clip large symbols.
\item \textbf{Fonts.} Math detection relies on font names; unusual or bitmap
  math fonts may be misclassified.
\item \textbf{Self-proofreading.} Without a source, the same model transcribes
  and proofreads; sheets cover numbered displays only.
\item \textbf{Unverified assumptions.} How Projects process PDF pages, and all
  token figures, are modelled rather than measured.
\end{itemize}

\section{Installation and requirements}

Upload \file{pdf-to-markdown.zip} under \emph{Customize $\rightarrow$ Skills} with
code execution enabled, replacing any earlier version. The scripts need PyMuPDF
and Pillow; \file{verify\_eqs.py} needs pdflatex; \file{check.py} needs Node.js and
installs KaTeX itself. All were present in Claude's sandbox during testing.

\section{Planned next}

Column support for strips, equation numbers and proofreading crops, followed by
a full two-column conversion; scan detection and a scanned-page test; dotted
equation numbers; placeholder panels and better crops on the proofreading
sheets; other caption placements for figures; a metered comparison of Markdown
and original PDF in a real Project; the Marker comparison, run locally.
